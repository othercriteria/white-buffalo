*August the Fifth, Eighteen Fifty-Eight*

It is done. I will set it down once, plainly, because this journal is sworn to true things and there is no one else to write them.

I went down in the gray hour before light. I had watched them long enough to know the shape of their days better than I know my own. The man's day began in the barn, alone, before dawn, tending his animals while the house slept, and his guns stayed in the house, because a man does not carry a rifle to pitch hay. He was faithful to that work the way other men are faithful to prayer, and that is where I found him, bent to it, his back to the door. He never knew I was in the world. It was quickly done — quicker than the war taught me to expect, for the war never let me choose my hour. It was not a fight. I had not thought it would be. I stood with him in the dark until I was sure, and the animals shifted in their stalls and went still again, and that was all there was.

The woman rose with the light and built her fire. I watched the smoke start from the pipe, and I went in at the door. She was quicker than her husband, and the room was hers, and she fought me with what it gave her. She reached the door twice. It was not quickly done. I will not write it further than that.

Afterward the kettle she had set was at the boil. That was the whole of the house for a while — the fire she had built still burning, the kettle going on with its work, as if the morning were an ordinary morning and someone would come to tend it. I stood in the doorway a long time. I did not know I would feel this. I thought I was past feeling.

I bore them nothing. Not hatred, not sympathy, nothing with a name to it. They could see the valley from their door, and she is in the valley. That is the whole of it, and writing it a second time will not make it more than it is.

*August the Sixth*

I dug the graves behind the barn, where the animals' trampling keeps the ground soft. I dug the first through the whole of the day and the second into the next morning, deep, because a shallow grave wants a mound of stone over it, and a mound is the first thing the wolves try. Dug deep, the stones can lie flat, one close course of them, and nothing will dig, and the frost will not heave them.

I carried them from the house one at a time and laid them in and filled the graves and carried the stones up from the creek and set them over, flat and close, a course of pavement over each. It took the two days. I was not quick at it and I was not trying to be.

I opened the stalls and drove the animals out. They stood about the yard awhile, and then they went. I took nothing from the house. Their flour is theirs. What the wolves leave is left; what this leaves is not.

*August the Seventh*

Today I made the house right. I took up the stained earth by the door and tamped fresh in its place. I set the broken chair in the corner, the way a man sets a thing he means to mend. I made the bed square, and wiped the table, and when I left I shut the door so the latch caught. I do not know who any of it was for. No one is coming. I did it anyway, and I was slow at it, and I was not sorry to be slow.

The herd moved north in the evening. She was at the head of them. I followed.
