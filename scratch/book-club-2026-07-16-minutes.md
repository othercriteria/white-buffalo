**White Buffalo — Book Club Minutes, July meeting**
Eight present. Wine: yes. Facilitator's record follows.

**First reactions.** Samantha (our book-a-week reader) opened by saying she read it in two sittings and cried at a crossed-out letter, "which has never happened to me over punctuation." Kevin, our thriller guy, admitted he came in expecting to complain and couldn't: "It's a manhunt. It's literally a hired-gun-tracks-a-killer plot wearing a literary coat. I was fine." Arlene (retired history teacher) loved it with reservations she'd get to. Priya (librarian) called it "a novella that knows it's a novella — nothing wasted." Marcus, our lapsed English major, said "it's Moby-Dick walked backwards — Ahab doesn't want to kill the white whale, he wants the whale to kill him," and was told he had to put a dollar in the jar every additional time he said Moby-Dick. Dana (nurse) liked it but read the whole second half as a medical chart — more below. Joellen, who grew up outside Valentine, Nebraska, said flatly: "Whoever wrote this did their homework, and I checked." And Rich did not finish. He stopped in the winter journal entries — "the one where the guy eats frozen wolf leftovers and it's negative forty for six pages" (08-journal-entry-iv.md, "I endure because I must") — and cheerfully asked us to spoil it for him.

**What happened at the homestead.** This took twenty minutes, because we did not all agree, and Rich was the reason. Having quit early, he assumed the husband did it — "the whole first chapter sets that up, the father says 'I think her husband killed her'" (01-chapter-one.md). Kevin walked him through it: Morrow, the journal-writer, murdered George and Catherine Hardin because the herd had drifted within a mile of their claim and they might notice the white buffalo (14-journal-entry-vii.md, "The settlers must be removed"). Then Samantha pointed out something half the room had missed: **the murder itself is never shown.** Journal VIII opens after the fact — "I have written of what was done in it, and I will not write of it again" (16-journal-entry-viii.md) — and the entry Farrell actually reads, the one where Morrow says "I did not know I would feel this" (19-chapter-ten.md), is withheld from us. Kevin confessed he'd flipped back looking for the missing entry, convinced his copy had a gap. Priya thought the withholding was the best decision in the book; Rich thought it was "a cheat." Arlene also cleared up a structural confusion several people admitted to: the journal starts in April 1856 and the tracker starts in November 1858, so for most of the book the two stories are two and a half years apart, converging. Dana said she'd read the first half thinking they were simultaneous and "kept waiting for them to pass each other."

**The two men.** Preference split roughly five (Farrell) to three (Morrow). The Farrell camp liked the procedure — the ice crossing, the failing horse, the cottonwood bark (15-chapter-eight.md). Dana, our fixator-on-a-detail, gave an unsolicited lecture: "The withdrawal arc is *correct*. Tremor, then sweats, then hallucinosis, then a full seizure on day four — 'a full-body convulsion that seized me' — that's the actual clinical timeline for delirium tremens, and the author even has him rationing." Nobody else had clocked it; she was very pleased. Joellen's detail: the trading-post whiskey going to slush in the cold while the Fort Kearny bottles stayed liquid (09-chapter-five.md) — "that's how you catch a trader watering his stock, and the book just leaves it there for you. That's when I trusted it." On similarity: Marcus argued the two men are nearly the same person — both Mexican War veterans, both hollowed out, one medicating with whiskey and one with a death wish — "and I couldn't always tell the voices apart in the middle." Priya pushed back hard: the sameness is the point, and the book says so — "He had gone looking for something large enough to swallow it whole; I had poured whiskey into it for ten years, a glass at a time. That was the difference, and I did not like how small it was" (17-chapter-nine.md). Marcus conceded the theme but held that Morrow's confession pages "sound like Farrell wrote them." Two others agreed; five didn't.

**Morrow: did he get what he wanted?** The genuine fight of the night. Recorded split: **four say yes, three say the book says no, Rich abstained** ("I wasn't there for the death, seems wrong to vote"). The yes camp (Kevin, Samantha, Dana, Joellen): he wanted to die by the sacred animal and he did — his face was calm, and he went "willingly, gladly" (16-journal-entry-viii.md). Kevin: "By the standard of the character's own goal, mission accomplished." The no camp (Priya, Marcus, Arlene) had the text. Marcus: Morrow himself calls it "vanity... Pride dressed up as penance" (06-journal-entry-iii.md). Priya: he had to *manufacture* the death — "Or I will have chosen it for her, and called it hers. I have decided that it comes to the same thing" (16-journal-entry-viii.md) — "he doesn't receive a sacred death, he stages one and does the paperwork himself." And Arlene landed the line the yes camp couldn't answer, Farrell reading the journal: "He had taught himself that she was giving" (19-chapter-ten.md) — the book's own verdict that Morrow took a meaning, the way he took everything else, the way the front matter says the book is *about* a man who takes what isn't his. Dana switched sides at that point, making it 3–4. Samantha held: "It can be delusional *and* he still got what he wanted. Those aren't exclusive."

**Catherine.** Samantha's territory. The crossed-out draft undid her: "You asked after children. George says there will be time for children when the place can keep them... he laughs and is not moved" — struck through with a single line, nothing started in its place (11-chapter-six.md). "That's a whole marriage in four sentences she couldn't send." Arlene noted the daguerreotype detail from the same chapter — Farrell thinks "She looked like her father. I did not tell him this" — as the book's driest kindness. Priya raised the objection: Catherine never exists in her own present tense — letters, a photograph, then a hallucination — "she's a device three times over." Marcus answered that her final appearance cuts against that: in the last chapter she stands beside her murderer's body "watching the white buffalo without wonder" (20-chapter-eleven.md) — "even Farrell's guilt can't make her perform awe for the thing that killed her. She refuses the myth." The room liked that better than Marcus expected.

**The ending.** Kevin announced he'd read the last two pages three times and changed his mind twice. First read: Farrell dies kneeling in the snow — no food for two days, DTs, "nothing left to follow." Second read: he lives, because the passage inventories his way home — the horse, the Hardins' supplies, the hundred dollars owed — "He would carry me, if I could make myself move" (20-chapter-eleven.md). Third read: "the book refuses to say, and I've decided I'm angry at how much I respect that." Dana, clinically: he's post-withdrawal peak, day six or seven; physically he can survive; the question is only volition. Joellen, practically: "The homestead's stocked. He makes it. Nebraska people know exactly how far that is." Priya read it as the real ending of the manhunt: Farrell has spent the book borrowing purpose — Aldridge's money, then Morrow's trail — "and when the buffalo goes over the ridge there's no one left to follow. Kneeling is what a man looks like when the last borrowed purpose walks off." Rich said it reminded him of the end of *The Revenant*, "the staring-into-camera part," and Kevin said it was more like a *Yellowstone* cold open with the sound off, and the comparison thread was gently killed. Would anyone end it differently? Kevin originally wanted Farrell and Morrow to meet — "then I realized 'He had never known I was in the world' (20-chapter-eleven.md) is the meeting, and it's crueler." Samantha wanted one more chapter: Aldridge in Oswego receiving the answer. Arlene disagreed — "the book opens in his hands and deliberately never returns; the not-knowing is his sentence too."

**The Lakota.** Arlene raised it. No Lakota person speaks in the book; they watch from ridges, leave the staked cloth and the bound skulls above the wintering valley (16-journal-entry-viii.md, "They have been keeping this valley longer than I have been keeping her"), and at the end they simply take the buffalo home. Priya read the authors' note aloud — no invented ceremony, no Lakota speech, everything seen through the period record of white observers, "because that record is the only knowledge such men could have had" (00-front-matter.md). The table split respectfully. Marcus and Priya: the silence is the ethics — the two white narrators are unreliable *about* this land, and the riders' wordless exit says the story was never theirs to narrate. Joellen and Dana were less settled: restraint can still leave the Lakota as scenery, watchers without interiority. Arlene's synthesis carried the room: the riders are the only people in the book who act correctly the first time — no taking, no staging, no speech required — "and the one long look at Farrell, with 'no sign... of welcome or of warning,' is the most eloquent moment in it."

**Recommendation and ratings.** Recommend to the next club: 7 yes, 1 abstain (Rich, on principle, unfinished).
- Arlene: ★★★★½ — "Buena Vista and the bleeding-Kansas talk are right; I checked."
- Dana: ★★★★ — "The most accurate DTs I've read in fiction; docked half for Marcus being partly right about the voices, rounded up for the horse."
- Kevin: ★★★★ — "Anticlimax as a plot twist. I'll be recommending it to people who'll hate it."
- Priya: ★★★★★ — "Not a wasted page. The withheld murder is the book."
- Joellen: ★★★★ — "It knows what cottonwood bark is for. That's five stars; the buffalo growing that fast is minus one."
- Samantha: ★★★★★ — "A crossed-out letter broke my heart. Five."
- Marcus: ★★★★ — "Moby-Dick in reverse" (final dollar deposited in jar).
- Rich: ★★★ — "Great wine tonight. I'll read the ending now that I know the buffalo did it."
