# Fertility and family limitation, 1850s — research note

Commissioned 2026-07-11 (DK) to ground the George Hardin
children-deferral pin before drafting. Web-verified sweep, eight
claims; full citations in the session transcript. Condensed verdicts
and book implications below.

## Findings

1. **Transition timing.** US white TFR 7.04 (1800) → 5.42 (1850) →
   5.21 (1860); decline uniquely early among nations except France
   (Haines, EH.net / NBER). Qualification: Hacker (Demography 2003)
   argues sustained decline only from c. 1840 and that pre-1860
   decline was mostly NUPTIALITY (later/less marriage), with marital
   fertility control not clearly national until after the Civil War.
   The field is genuinely split (Yasuba–Easterlin–Haines vs Hacker).

2. **The gradient.** Yasuba (1962) / Easterlin (JEH): child-woman
   ratios track land availability; frontier fertility decisively
   highest, settled East lowest. Native-born Protestant middle-class
   upstate New York was the national leading edge of deliberate
   limitation (Haines & Guest 2008; Ryan, *Cradle of the Middle
   Class* — Utica/Oneida County, the closest portrait of CATHERINE'S
   natal culture). Ohio farm country intermediate.

3. **Means in circulation.** Withdrawal the workhorse (Owen, *Moral
   Physiology* 1831; Brodie 1994); Knowlton 1832 (douching); Hollick
   (1845/1850) reached thousands of households and **lectured along
   the Ohio** — i.e., George's demographic was reachable. Abortion
   rising to ~1 per 5–6 live births by the 1850s–60s (Mohr 1978).
   Rubber condoms only from 1855, marginal and dear.

4. **First-birth deferral was NOT a thing.** No historian attributes
   any of the decline to postponing first births; control was
   stopping and spacing, AFTER childbearing began (Sanderson 1979;
   Anderton & Bean 1985; Tolnay & Guest 1984). First births
   overwhelmingly within 9–24 months of the wedding; ~10% of 1850
   brides pregnant at the altar (Smith & Hindus 1975). Three
   childless years read as barrenness or divine affliction — real
   stigma, even divorce-suit precedent. Frontier couples of the
   1850s were essentially natural-fertility populations (Bean,
   Mineau & Anderton, *Fertility Change on the American Frontier*).

5. **The prudential displacement (the key finding).** Malthusian
   "moral restraint" was mainstream respectable discourse (Alcott,
   *The Young Man's Guide*), and delaying-for-a-farm was real
   practice — but the respectable script attached prudence to
   MARRIAGE TIMING ("don't take a wife until you're established"),
   not to childlessness within marriage. Deferring children INSIDE a
   marriage was the freethinker register (Owen/Knowlton/Hollick) —
   available to a literate man, voiced privately to a wife, not at a
   raising. No emigrant guide counsels married couples to defer
   children until the claim proves.

6. **Domestic feminism.** In Smith's (1973) account, within-marriage
   limitation was often the WIFE'S project (marital abstinence,
   domestic power). Given Catherine's leading-edge Oswego origins,
   whose policy the deferral quietly is — his, hers, negotiated — is
   a historically live question.

7. **REFUTED motif — do not use:** couples timing pregnancy around
   migration. Schlissel (1982) concludes the opposite: "a woman's
   pregnancy mattered very little to emigrant families... not
   sufficient cause to defer the trip." One in five overland women
   pregnant or birthing en route. The childbirth-in-isolation FEAR,
   by contrast, is among the best-documented anxieties in frontier
   women's writing (Faragher; Schlissel; Riley) — usable freely.

## Implications for the book

- **The line is sayable; the register is private.** George
  articulating wait-until-the-place-can-keep-them is period-credible
  as a thing said to his wife — touching foresight or suspicious
  eccentricity to period ears, "usable tension, not anachronism."
  It is NOT a thing said publicly or written to a father-in-law.
- **Therefore: the deferral belongs in an UNSENT DRAFT, not a sent
  letter.** The sent letters' silence on children (03:11, canon) is
  itself period-true behavior — the subject carries stigma east.
  Catherine's unsent drafts at the homestead (11:95-101) are exactly
  where the unsendable truth lives. This preserves Farrell's Ch 2
  observation intact and explains it.
- **Aldridge's misreading is period-correct.** "Its own kind of
  answer" (01:63) — reading three childless years as marriage
  trouble/affliction is what his world would do. No repair needed;
  the research ratifies existing canon.
- **Catherine's agency can live in the draft.** The domestic-
  feminism finding licenses her pushing back in her own unsent
  words — deferral as contested household policy, George the holder.
  Her natal culture makes her the one most likely to KNOW limitation
  was possible; the book need never say how. Mechanism stays
  unstated, as the period would keep it.
- **George stays coherent:** a man who prices risk and defers family
  until the position proves is the freethinker-adjacent, figuring
  kind — consistent with almanac marginalia and powder purchases.
  The atypicality is the point: he is an atypical settler (beyond
  the line) making an atypical private calculation, and the couple
  bears the social reading of misfortune, which the book already
  shows (Aldridge).
