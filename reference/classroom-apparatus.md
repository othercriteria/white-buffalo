# Classroom apparatus (from the 2026-07-11 teacher exercises)

Distilled from two fresh-context agent runs: an 11th-grade literature
teacher (unit + honest student simulation + grade) and a U.S. History
teacher (usability audit + DBQ). Durable material for any future
teacher's guide, submission packet, or marketing claim of classroom
fitness. The full reports live in the session transcripts; this file
keeps what outlives them.

## Teachability verdict

Both teachers: assignable at 11th grade — the lit teacher whole-book
with framing, the history teacher excerpted (Ch 1-4 + JE I; JE VII;
JE IX + Ch 10-11) with apparatus. "Nothing here disqualifies it;
everything here obligates the teacher."

## The sensitive-content protocol (lit teacher's four parts)

1. Front-load the content naming on day one — never let students meet
   the suicide theme alone at home in JE I.
2. Teach the journal as a self-justifying document BEFORE reading it
   ("what is this narrator selling, and to whom?").
3. Counseling-office coordination; safe-messaging practice — discuss
   the character's reasoning as reasoning, never debate whether he was
   "right."
4. Parent notification + a genuine alternative text, offered without
   ceremony.
With these, no passage cut: "cutting Journal IX would gut the unit's
whole point, which is learning to withstand a persuasive voice."
Comparables already taught at this level: Hamlet, Of Mice and Men,
The Things They Carried, Ethan Frome.

## The naive-reader misread map (lit teacher; evidence for stack #29)

A strong 17-year-old reads the ending as redemption ("finally forgives
himself") despite "It was not forgiveness" on the page, and JE IX as
deathbed remorse rather than rationalization completing itself. "The
book's rebuttal is structural... structural rebuttals are exactly what
struggling 16-year-olds don't hear." Predicted misreads: JE IX as
redemption; the peace as healing; the buffalo's inner life as fact;
JE VII as genuine entrapment (the sharpest students argue Morrow's
side — by design); "I made it fit" read as a transition sentence; the
Lakota as mystical set dressing.

## The false-model corrections (history teacher, ranked)

1. Settlement pattern — the big one: assigned cold, students absorb
   "Little House backdated fifteen years." The book marks the Hardins
   as anomalous (trader's warning, SIOUX across the blank map,
   "further out than sense"); a teacher still opens with an 1858
   settlement map and lets students find the Niobrara on it.
2. Lakota sovereignty inversion: the aggregate impression is a power
   vacuum with Indians as weather; in truth 1856-58 plains were the
   core of a functioning Lakota world near its height (Hämäläinen).
3. Violence type: the book corrects the arrow-death myth (cold, ice,
   drink kill instead) but substitutes the anarchic-white-drifter
   archetype; systemic violence (armies, militias, dispossession) is
   the real period pattern.
4. Absences: Panic of 1857 (now one clause, 05 gold passage); the
   Kansas-Nebraska Act as the territory's reason for existing;
   Pawnee/Ponca/Omaha country walked through unnamed (Ponca lived on
   the LOWER Niobrara — east of the book's ground; the Loup forks
   are pressed-Pawnee/contested range — see stack #35).

## Representation: how a 2026 classroom teaches the Lakota silence

Both cases stated honestly. For restraint: white narrators in 1856-59
could know the Lakota only as trader's talk and distant sightings;
the Lakota are the book's only competent actors (read the situation
first, mark it, monitor it, receive the buffalo); "structurally, the
ending says: this was their story all along and the narrators were
trespassers in it." For erasure: watchful silent horsemen are the
oldest scenery-Indian trope; the plot borrows Ptesáŋwiŋ for a white
man's death without one Lakota word. Teaching move: the hole is the
assignment — students fill it from real sources.

## Pairing shelf (history teacher's source list)

- Winter counts (Lone Dog, Battiste Good; Smithsonian) — the 1855-56
  entries against Morrow's walking years.
- Blue Water, Lakota side: Cokawin's account in Bettelyoun &
  Waggoner, *With My Own Eyes*; vs Harney's report and Lt. G. K.
  Warren's journal (Warren then surveyed this book's exact country,
  1855-57).
- Recorded Lakota speech: Fort Pierre council minutes (1856); Bear's
  Rib speeches.
- Ptesáŋwiŋ as Lakota tell it: Black Elk in J. E. Brown, *The Sacred
  Pipe*; Arvol Looking Horse on the living bundle.
- Framing: Hämäläinen, *Lakota America*; Ostler, *The Plains Sioux
  and U.S. Colonialism*.
- Settler women's own words: Mollie Dorsey Sanford's diary (Nebraska
  Territory, 1857-58) — pair with Catherine's unfinished draft.
- Rock Island: press reports of Lincoln's Hurd v. Rock Island closing
  (Chicago Daily Press, Sept 1857).

## DBQ set (ready-made)

1. Ferry/bridge passage + Lincoln's Hurd argument: which way was the
   economy turning by 1857, and who stood to lose?
2. Catherine's unfinished draft + Sanford's diary: what did women in
   the territory say and leave unsaid to the families behind them?
3. The two Blue Water mentions (sutler vs Creighton) + Warren's
   journal + a winter-count entry: four accounts of one event —
   account for the differences; what does "restless" conceal?

## Positioning line (both teachers converged)

The Mexican War as the psychic prehistory of the Civil War generation
— "the best classroom entry into war trauma and the 1850s I've
encountered" — and ambient history arriving the way it actually
arrived: "sideways, in taverns, garbled. That's a skill textbooks
can't teach: hearing the period's noise."
