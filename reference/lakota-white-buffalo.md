# Lakota and the White Buffalo

Research compiled for *White Buffalo* revision. The ending involves Lakota riders and the sacred white buffalo.

## White Buffalo Calf Woman (Ptesáŋwiŋ)

**Who She Is:**
- Lakȟótiyapi: Ptesáŋwiŋ (White Buffalo Calf Woman) or White Buffalo Maiden
- "A supernatural entity of the Sioux, who serves as an intermediary between the people and the Great Spirit"
- "Central to the Lakota religion as the primary cultural prophet"
- Appeared during a time of famine, when people had "forgotten how to pray and had lost connection with the Great Spirit"

**The Legend:**
- 19 generations ago, during famine, a chief sent two scouts to hunt
- They encounter a beautiful maiden in the wilderness
- One scout is "lustful" and tries to lay hands on her — he is killed
- The other shows "proper respect" and is sent to tell his people she is coming
- She arrives and brings sacred gifts

**What She Brought:**
- The čhaŋnúŋpa — the sacred ceremonial pipe
- The Seven Sacred Rites

**The Seven Sacred Rites:**
1. Purifying body and spirit (sweat lodge)
2. The Sun Dance
3. The vision quest
4. Making of relatives
5. Throwing of the ball
6. Preparing a girl for womanhood
7. Keeping of the spirit

**Her Departure (key for the novel):**
- As she left, she stopped, lay down, and rolled over
- First became a black buffalo
- Then a red buffalo
- Then a yellow buckskin buffalo
- Finally, a white buffalo
- Then "walking out of sight"

## The Sacred White Buffalo

**Significance:**
- "The most sacred living thing on earth" to American Indians
- "A profoundly significant spiritual event"
- A sign of hope
- A reminder of the covenant between Lakota and buffalo nation
- "A sign to begin life's sacred loop"

**Rarity:**
- Extremely rare
- The birth of one is interpreted as a message
- Both blessing and warning

**Recent Context (2024):**
- A white buffalo calf born in Yellowstone (June 4, 2024)
- Lakota chief Arvol Looking Horse: "The birth of this calf is both a blessing and warning. We must do more."

## Lakota in the 1850s

**Territory:**
- Settled in Black Hills region around 1765
- Moved from Minnesota to western South Dakota, eastern Wyoming, eastern Montana
- Conquered Cheyenne in 1776, took Black Hills territory
- Black Hills = center of their culture

**Bands:**
- The Brulé (Sičháŋǧu) — one of the major bands
- "The Sicangu centered their territories around the White River, from its mouth to the margins of the Black Hills"
- Other bands include Oglala, Miniconjou, Hunkpapa, etc.

**1850s Context:**
- "The tribes were thriving and there were plenty of buffalo"
- But gold discovery bringing white settlers to the plains
- 1857: "Thousands of Sioux gathered in the Black Hills and vowed to protect their lands from the whites"
- Both whites and Indians recognized buffalo were disappearing already

**Lifestyle:**
- "Did not build permanent shelters but instead roamed their land"
- But population remained within identifiable domain
- Black Hills important for lodge poles, plant resources, small game

**Winter Patterns:**
- Would seek shelter in protected areas
- Black Hills provided resources and protection
- Moved seasonally following buffalo and resources

## The Sacred Pipe Today

- Kept by Arvol Looking Horse (b. 1954), 19th keeper
- Passed down through lineage for 19 generations
- Original pipe from White Buffalo Calf Woman still exists

## The First Sioux War and the Dictated Peace (researched 2026-07-08 — the gap's answer)

**The timeline lock.** The novel runs April 1856 – January 1859. Blue Water Creek
(Battle of Ash Hollow) was September 3, 1855: Harney's punitive expedition killed
86 of Little Thunder's Brulé — women and children among them — north of the Platte
in present Garden County, Nebraska, i.e., the country just southwest of the book's
setting. Little Thunder, Spotted Tail, and Iron Shell tried to parley under a white
flag; Harney refused. In March 1856 Harney summoned the leaders of the seven Lakota
bands to Fort Pierre and dictated terms — "essentially, obedience and docility" —
releasing his Blue Water prisoners in exchange. What followed was roughly ten years
of restrained peace in which the Lakota "tried to ignore" the emigrants crossing
their land.

**What this means for the book:** Morrow first sees the calf seven months after the
massacre and one month after the Fort Pierre council. The Brulé bands of this exact
country spent 1856–58 keeping deliberate distance from white men — grieving,
watched by the Army, under dictated terms, with their own leaders (Spotted Tail
foremost, after his surrender and imprisonment) counseling restraint. A band
observing a wakan animal shadowed by a solitary white man has, in this precise
window, every historical reason to watch and wait rather than approach: approaching
her means dealing with him, and dealing with any white man is what the peace
forbids and prudence rejects. **The three-year distance is not thin; it is 1856.**
The text already carries the seed — the Fort Kearny sutler's "Sioux been restless
since Blue Water" (07:97) — the white man's obtuse reading of a people keeping wide.

## Historical treatment of actual white buffalo

- White hides were supreme ceremonial objects plains-wide. Catlin (1832) saw a
  white buffalo robe raised on a pole in the Mandan village as an offering to the
  Great Spirit — killed by Blackfeet, purchased by the Mandans for eight horses
  and a quantity of goods. In the early 1800s "few Indians would part with white
  or partly white buffalo robes."
- So a living white cow was not automatically inviolate everywhere — some
  traditions took the animal ceremonially for the consecrated hide. Treatment
  varied by nation and by the animal (a cow vs. bull, calf vs. grown).
- For the novel this means leaving her alive and attended is a CHOICE with weight,
  not a default — which the Ptesáŋwiŋ tradition (the living calf as her sign, per
  the departure story: black, red, yellow, white) and the post-Harney moment both
  support. The ending — leading her away live toward the sacred places — sits
  inside the reverence-for-the-living-sign tradition.
- Offerings of cloth and goods to wakan things/places are attested practice;
  visible, legible-at-distance acts of attendance are available to the narrators'
  eyes without any interiority.

## Winter counts

Lakota winter counts (Lone Dog's, High Dog's, Iron Hawk's, etc.) named each year
for its most notable event; buffalo-related events and ceremonies appear
throughout (e.g., Iron Hawk 1824, the whitened buffalo-horn ceremony).

**Verified (2026-07-08):** *White Cow Killer* was a real Oglala winter-count
keeper — his count was collected by Corbusier and used in Mallery's Smithsonian
comparative work ("White Cow Killer calls it..." runs through the BAE tables,
e.g., his names for 1825-26 and 1828-29). A Lakota man named for killing a white
buffalo cow, entrusted with his band's calendar: this verifies at once that white
cows were killed by Lakota men, that the act was significant enough to confer a
name, and that the white-buffalo and winter-count traditions intersect in a
documented person. Also: Lone Dog's count names 1850-51 for a wondrous buffalo
event ("buffalo cow was killed and an old woman found in her belly") — five years
before this story; buffalo wonders were exactly winter-count material. For
count-by-count detail, the full source is Greene & Thornton, *The Year the Stars
Fell: Lakota Winter Counts at the Smithsonian* (2007).

Note: neither narrator could know any of this — it is background truth for the
authors, not text. It means the Lakota's record of her would predate and outlast
both men's accounts; the book's two documents are not the only documents. (And
White Cow Killer's existence sharpens the book's central tension: killing her was
a real and honored practice — the choice to attend her instead, in the post-Harney
years, carries that much more weight.)

## Application to the Novel

**What the Lakota Would See:**
- A white buffalo is sacred beyond measure
- They would approach with reverence
- They would want to take her to sacred places (Black Hills)
- A white man following her = strange, possibly blasphemous
- A white man killed by her = possibly fitting, possibly tragic

**Restraint for the Novel:**
- Don't over-explain
- Don't put modern spiritual language in their mouths
- Show their actions, not their theology
- The reverence should be evident in behavior
- Farrell wouldn't understand what he's seeing

**Language:**
- Ptesáŋwiŋ = White Buffalo Woman/Maiden (already used in draft)
- Sičháŋǧu = Brulé band
- Čhaŋnúŋpa = sacred pipe
- Wakan Tanka = Great Mystery / Great Spirit

**The Ending:**
- Lakota riders leading white buffalo away = returning sacred thing to sacred place
- They don't save Farrell = he's not their responsibility
- They don't acknowledge him = he's irrelevant to what matters
- The white buffalo going "into the darkness and was gone" = appropriate, mysterious
