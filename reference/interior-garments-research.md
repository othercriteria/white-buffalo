# Interior Garments Research — 1850s Frontier Home (Nebraska, ~1855-58)

Visual-accuracy notes for illustration. Physical/visual facts only. Compiled 2026-07.

## 1. How garments were hung/stored

### No coat hangers existed yet
- The shoulder-shaped hanger did not exist in the 1850s. The earliest related patent is
  O. A. North's improved clothes **hook** (US Patent #85,756, Jan 12, 1869, New Britain CT).
  The recognizable twisted-wire shoulder hanger was improvised by Albert J. Parkhouse in
  1903 (Timberlake Wire & Novelty Co., Jackson MI); patent #822,981 issued 1906.
  Mass adoption follows in the 1900s-1910s.
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clothes_hanger
  - http://theinventors.org/library/inventors/blcloathanger.htm
- "Clothes-shoulders"/clothesholders only begin appearing in ads in the late 1890s.
  Coat hangers were effectively unknown until the very end of the 19th century.
  - https://johnstoncollection.org/Development-of-Clothing-Storage~65146

### Period practice
- Clothes were hung on **pegs or hooks** (in wardrobes for the well-off, on walls for
  everyone else) or **folded flat** in chests, trunks, and drawers. Peasant/settler
  households used chests and wall pegs, not wardrobes.
  - https://johnstoncollection.org/Development-of-Clothing-Storage~65146
- Garments hung from hooks via a **hanging loop** — a short bar of fabric (or chain) sewn
  inside at the nape of the collar. Standard on men's coats through the 19th c.; the loop
  exists precisely because wardrobes had hooks, not rods.
  - https://forums.vintagefashionguild.org/threads/hanger-loops-on-vintage-clothing.79856/
- Most clothing, especially women's dresses and anything not in daily rotation, was
  **folded and stored in a trunk or chest**; hanging was for garments in active use
  (coats, cloaks, hats, aprons, everyday work clothes).
  - https://johnstoncollection.org/Development-of-Clothing-Storage~65146

### Settler cabin specifics
- Cabin furnishings were sparse: benches, a bedstead, a table, and "a cupboard or chest
  for storing bedding and clothing." Iron nails were expensive on the frontier; **whittled
  wooden pegs driven into the log walls** (also used as loft ladders) were the default
  fastener — so wall pegs, forged hooks, or a nail here and there, not a milled peg rail.
  - https://www.motherbedford.com/LogCabin.htm
  - https://www.ducksters.com/history/westward_expansion/log_cabin.php
- A formal Shaker-style peg *rail* (board with turned pegs) is plausible only in a more
  finished frame house; in a first-generation cabin, individual pegs in the wall log or
  in a rough board are more credible.

### What a dress on a wall peg actually looks like
- It does NOT show shoulder shape. Hung by its collar loop or with the neckline/waistband
  hooked over the peg, the garment hangs from a single point: fabric bunches at the peg,
  shoulders collapse inward and fold down, and everything tapers out to the skirt below —
  a long teardrop/cone of cloth, often somewhat crumpled. Alternatively a skirt alone
  hangs by waistband loops, or a garment is simply draped/folded over the peg.

## 2. The garments themselves

### Woman's everyday dress, 1855-58
- **Bodice**: fitted, darted (or pleated) to the body, lined; **high neckline**, often
  finished with a small white collar; waistline at the natural waist by mid-decade
  (round or slightly pointed), joined to the skirt with a **piped waist seam** — the
  single strongest visual marker vs. a modern dress.
  - https://fashionhistory.fitnyc.edu/1850-1859/
  - https://pastpatterns.com/pattern/1850s-dart-fitted-bodice-sewing-pattern/
- **Skirt**: straight widths of fabric **gauged (cartridge-pleated) or knife-pleated**
  into the waist seam — dense tiny pleats throwing the volume out abruptly from the
  waist. A surviving ca. 1850 calico dress used *seven fabric widths* pleated to the
  waist: hem circumference on the order of 4-5 yards. Skirt reaches the floor or ankle
  (work length slightly shorter).
  - https://fashionhistory.fitnyc.edu/1840-1849/ (gauging technique)
  - https://insideoutksum.wordpress.com/19th-century/printed-day-dress-ca-1850/
- **Sleeves**: long. Fashion plates show widening **pagoda sleeves** (widest 1857-60)
  with white undersleeves; a work dress more likely has plain full sleeves gathered to
  a cuff (bishop) or a simple coat sleeve.
  - https://fashionhistory.fitnyc.edu/1850-1859/
- **Underpinnings**: dome shape came from **multiple petticoats (up to seven)**, one
  horsehair-stiffened; the **cage crinoline (steel hoops) arrives only in 1856** and a
  frontier work dress would be worn over petticoats alone. Off the body, none of that
  structure is present — the skirt is just yards of limp fabric.
  - https://fashionhistory.fitnyc.edu/1850-1859/
- **Fabric**: everyday = printed cotton calico (small dark figured prints), wool, or
  homespun; dark/medium colors that hide dirt. Flounced patterned silks are fashion-plate
  wear, not frontier work wear.
  - https://insideoutksum.wordpress.com/19th-century/printed-day-dress-ca-1850/
- Bodice and skirt are one garment (front-closing with hooks or buttons for a work
  dress); no visible zipper, no waist elastic, no separate blouse-and-skirt look yet.

### Man's coat on a peg
- **Sack coat**: the 1850s everyman's coat — loose, unfitted, **no waist seam or back
  shaping**, hip/upper-thigh length, small collar, 3-4 buttons (often worn buttoned only
  at top), wool (heavier or lighter weight).
  - https://lilyabsinthe.com/a-brief-view-of-mens-clothing-the-sack-coat-2/
- **Frock coat**: fitted, waist seam, full knee-length skirt all around, center back
  vent; heavy wool broadcloth. Dressier — a settler might own one for Sunday.
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frock_coat
- On the peg it hangs by the collar loop at the nape: collar bunched at the peg,
  shoulders fallen slack, sleeves hanging flat against the body of the coat.

## Prompt-relevant conclusions

- **Never depict a coat hanger** (wood or wire) — anachronistic by ~50 years. Garments
  hang directly on whittled wooden pegs, forged iron hooks, or nails in the log wall.
- No closet, no rod, no peg-rail millwork: a row of rough pegs in the wall, or one or
  two pegs/nails near the door and bed. Most clothing lives folded in a wooden trunk
  or chest (which can sit visibly in frame).
- A hanging dress = single-point hang: fabric gathered/bunched at the peg, **no shoulder
  silhouette**, collapsing into a long full cone of cloth; slightly crumpled is correct.
- Dress cues: high neck, fitted darted bodice, piped seam at the natural waist, skirt
  densely pleated/gauged into that seam, enormous hem (4-5 yards around) pooling limp
  without its petticoats, long plain sleeves. Dark small-print calico or plain wool.
- No hoops visible or implied for a frontier work dress pre-/circa-1856; fullness comes
  from fabric alone when hanging.
- Man's coat: loose thigh-length wool sack coat (or knee-length frock for Sunday),
  hanging slack from a nape loop, shoulders collapsed.
