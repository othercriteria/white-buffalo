# Homestead construction realities (research 2026-07-09)

Two-person build feasibility, upper Niobrara, spring 1857 - Aug 1858
(16 months, one winter). Commissioned after DK flagged construction
ignorance during the graves plate. Full agent report in session
transcript; verdicts and text-audit results below.

## Verdicts vs the text — the manuscript passes

- Lean-to "barely large enough for a horse and a cow" (11:53) = exactly
  the feasible three-sided sod-and-pole stable. Frame/large barn would
  have been impossible (no sawmill within ~150 mi).
- Well with "wooden frame... pulley and rope" (11:53) = correct windlass
  rig; shallow water table near the creek (10-25 ft); no windmill
  (1870s+). Caving sand means willow cribbing — unstated in text, fine.
- Root cellar in the hillside (11:103): easiest build on the claim.
- One small window (11:53): correct for glass scarcity (one small sash
  hauled from the river settlements, or oiled paper).
- Stovepipe through sod roof (11:79): period-correct; NO masonry
  chimney, no brick, no board floors, no shingles.
- "Fields" (14:81): realistic ceiling ~15-25 acres broken by Aug 1858
  (sod corn year one; first real crop maturing when they died — possibly
  never harvested). Text gives no numbers; compatible.
- Settlers-not-homesteaders (v0.7 fix): doubly correct — no Homestead
  Act until 1862; the Hardins are squatters/preemption claimants decades
  ahead of the settlement frontier (actual upper-Niobrara settlement:
  late 1870s-80s), on effectively unceded Lakota range. This IS the
  book's premise (isolation, no neighbors, nobody finds them).

## Setting-specific notes

- Timber IS available (cedar canyons, pine north slopes): good
  ridgepoles/rafters; the soddy needs no desperate-treelessness trope.
  Sod-house roof: low gable on a cedar ridgepole, willow/brush + hay +
  sod; sagging along the ridge at 16 months, grass sprouting from it,
  rain-channeled wall corners.
- Sod house build: 2-3 weeks of wall/roof work for two people (no
  neighbors for a raising); ~14x16 ft interior; walls 2-2.5 ft thick.
- **Cairn stones (the graves question): REALISTIC, geologically
  specific.** Sand hills proper are stone-free (no glacial erratics —
  the ice never reached them). But the Niobrara valley breaks expose
  hard, gray, opaline silica-cemented sandstone (basal Valentine Fm.) as
  slabby ledges and broken blocks along river and creek mouths — dense,
  weather-resistant, gatherable. Covering two graves means working the
  creek breaks and hauling by wagon or stone-boat: a day or two of labor
  per grave. Morrow's "stones placed just so" (13:17) cost real work —
  the care Farrell reads is heavier than the text says. Chalk is WRONG
  for this setting (Niobrara Fm. chalk is far downstream).
